STAR-01 · Daimler, Sr. Engineer, Electro Mobility Group

Discarded body-domain PHS HIL rig (transit damage, no PSU, no schematics) back in production in 3–5 weeks (₹2–3 Cr cost avoidance)

In 2022 we took on a discarded three-cabinet dSPACE PHS rig from Germany. Daimler India Commercial Vehicles needed HILs on a small budget, and our testing team had never owned a HIL, so restoring it served both. It had wiring shaken loose in transit, no main power supply and no schematics. A team of eight took it on as a part-time stretch assignment and rebuilt it bottom-up: backplane, power, processor, I/O boards, wiring. I planned the integration and testing of the first ECU we brought up: the EAM, the module that handles a customer's special lighting and body requirements. I also handled procurement. It was running again in three to five weeks, and we handed it over to the body control module team. It still serves DICV today. That avoided an estimated two to three crore.
STAR-02 · Daimler, Sr. Technical Lead

Ground loop on the 5-cabinet e-mobility HIL: 2–3 months of model reloads on high-torque steps

In 2025 our five-cabinet SCALEXIO HIL for the e-mobility powertrain kept reloading its models whenever a high-torque step demanded high current from the inverters. Five teams shared that rig, so testing kept getting hit. We spent two to three months ruling out controllers and plant models. Then our department's expert pointed to grounding. I coordinated dSPACE and our central hardware team; they found a ground loop and re-did the grounding around one common ground point. After that, the bench was stable.
STAR-03 · DTICI / Daimler, current role

HIL ML Ops POC: LLM + RAG test pipeline with human gates, from a virtual build to the dSPACE SCALEXIO rig

Our teams in India, Germany and the US were re-solving the same root causes, sometimes for up to three months. I built a proof of concept that turns requirements into tests, runs them on our five-cabinet dSPACE SCALEXIO rig through ECU-TEST and the XIL API, and drafts root causes with a RAG agent over Jira and Confluence. I built it virtually first with Claude Code, then moved it to the rig with Daimler IT's help, with GPT-4o and the RAG kept inside a sandboxed Daimler environment. The LLM only drafts a model of the requirements; code derives the tests and decides every verdict, a human signs off at each gate, and the team uses it today.
STAR-04 · Daimler, Sr. Engineer

Shift-left reviews & cross-team RCA/defect closure (ACC acceleration-request timeout, e-Torque, 2022)

In 2022, on e-Torque, a defect on adaptive cruise control had already bounced between teams once. The ADAS unit sends the e-Torque master controller an acceleration request, and we'd flagged how the controller reacted when that request timed out. We found it on the HIL; vehicle testing ran the same case on the truck, didn't see it, and pushed back. I ran the discussion in our weekly cross-level huddle; the component owner and system responsible agreed the intended behaviour, a controlled ramp to zero and hand-back to the driver, and we fixed the requirement so all three levels tested the same thing. It closed at the root cause instead of bouncing again.
STAR-05 · Daimler, current role, Apr 2023–present

Zero-delay start for 2026 e-mobility campaigns

Before every campaign I review the specifications and line up the lab infrastructure against the milestones, then track risk so nothing surfaces late. That's how all three of our major 2026 e-mobility campaigns started with zero delay, covering charging, battery management, e-torque, ACC and AEB. The closest call was a week before our R2601 campaign: an unannounced parameter change locked the driving scenario, and the HIL was down for three to four days. I mapped who owned what in the master controller, and with the thermal-module tester we traced it to the thermal-management ECU's signals. R2601 started on time, and since then every parameter change goes through our master parameter file.
STAR-06 · Daimler, Sr. Engineer, 2020–2023

Per-function verification cycle halved (~2 weeks → ~1 week)

I co-led verification for three major EV programmes, where one function took about two weeks to verify. STAR-07 · Daimler, current role

Traceability: DOORS → test cases → bugs, 100% requirement coverage

I built automated trace analysis linking DOORS requirements to test cases, vehicle logs and bug reports. Where a requirement had no test case, like a performance requirement, we pulled the signals straight from the CAN log MDF files and compared them with the expected output in the requirement. It flagged uncovered requirements, and it covered performance testing, not just functional and functional safety. On the tests derived with the standard I'd set up, we reached 100% requirement coverage at sign-off.
STAR-08 · Daimler, current role

Global Power BI KPI dashboard + India bench utilisation to 20–22 h/day

Our HIL benches were spread across India, Germany and the US, with a 13-hour time gap, and every bench had its own Outlook calendar. Nobody could see all of them at once, and utilisation wasn't even tracked. I architected a global Power BI dashboard, and my team built it: bench availability, ECU and test-environment configuration, requirement maturity and defect clusters, with self-service booking in one place. Testers could now pick the right bench in any country for their test scope, and India bench utilisation went up to 20 to 22 hours a day.
STAR-09 · Daimler, current role

Unified IRM + Systems Engineering framework for EMI/EMC (now company standard)

We kept seeing interference in the field: on the radio, on the mirror cams, on the reverse-parking camera in the cluster. The pattern was a gap between release management and systems engineering: suppliers were getting EMI/EMC requirements without the rationale behind them. Together with Daimler's global head of EMI/EMC testing, I analysed the cases and traced the root cause, then wrote a unified framework aligned to the global systems engineering process, so every EMI/EMC requirement now goes to suppliers with its rationale. He drove the adoption, and it's now the company standard.

STAR-11 · TATA Technologies / TATA Motors, Nov 2018–May 2020

Tata Motors: Tigor EV calibration, e-drive controller → ARAI, blower field fix

At Tata Motors, we got dead EV prototypes from the proto shop and brought them up for road-load testing: sensors and actuators first, then thermal management, charging, and finally drivability calibration, from creep to anti-jerk. I also validated a field fix: after long drives, the blower slowed down on its own at the same setting. A teammate found the cause and wrote a software-only control fix, because extra hardware in a packed chassis meant re-doing homologation. I tested it on the lab bench, then flashed it into the body control module and drove the car with the field engineer for two to three hours straight: the blower held its speed.
STAR-12 · TATA Motors, Nov 2018–May 2020

INCA/XCP live monitoring for RLDA readiness (Tata Motors)

At Tata Motors, before an EV prototype went into RLDA testing, I used ETAS INCA over XCP on the vehicle master controller to check every sensor and actuator. I didn't just watch signals: I drove the actuators through XCP parameters and confirmed the hardware physically responded: the HV contactors clicking, the pumps, fans and blower running. That caught real faults: more than once the contactor click didn't come, because the contactors had welded, which means high voltage is always connected. We did all of it with the car on a lift, wheels off the ground.
STAR-13 · Daimler, current role

CANape / VCDM Studio (CAN monitoring + parameterisation)

My most frequent use of VCDM Studio was on our shared five-cabinet SCALEXIO HIL. Many testing teams changed parameters in the common powertrain controller for their tests. The rule was to revert everything at the end of your slot, but very often that didn't happen. So we kept a master calibration set, updated from Germany with every software release. Whenever the HIL wasn't behaving, restoring it was our first step. About half the time that alone fixed it; the rest of the time we started debugging from a known state.
STAR-14 · personal project, 2026

DIY ADAS chassis: park assist, AEB-style stop, camera traffic-light stop

On my own time I built a small ADAS chassis. An ESP32-S3 runs a safety loop designed at 20 milliseconds: rear-ultrasonic park assist with graded zones, a hard stop at 15 centimetres with hysteresis, and a safe stop if that rear sensor fails while reversing. A front camera detects traffic lights on a PC and requests a stop on red; the controller latches it and only releases on a confirmed green. It's hobby-scale, not automotive-grade, but it's the same thinking as on a HIL: degraded modes, who owns the decision, and clean grounding.

Aarogya One Connect: solo-built clinic platform with voice AI + human approval

STAR-17 · Daimler Truck

Scaling the team from 2–3 to 30 engineers

I was the first engineer inducted into a new verification team, set up for a future battery-electric truck programme. That programme was slow to start, so we first supported a running project, and by the end we were carrying three programmes at once. Between 2021 and 2022 the team grew from two or three engineers to thirty. My part was onboarding people and setting the standard frameworks: a Function Understanding Document for every function, with integration diagrams, review gates and checklists. The hard part was getting juniors to accept it, because they found it too rigid. So we kept the framework open to their changes, but asked everyone to follow it strictly for their first two or three functions. By then they saw the benefit. And it did change: when the checklist grew to forty or fifty items, the whole team cut it to ten non-negotiable ones. Per-function verification time halved, and thirty people worked the same way.
STAR-18 · Daimler

Mentoring: onboarding engineers to the bench

I'm a certified mentor at Daimler. Every new joiner on our team followed the same path. First, the system: an IIT Madras NPTEL course on electric vehicles was mandatory. Then I trained them myself on three things: the network architecture of their vehicle variant, how the ECUs, sensors and actuators talk; the files, DBC and ARXML; and the PROVEtec tool. Alongside, sessions on the restbus and plant model. That took about a month. Then they shadowed a tester through one full campaign for about another month, reporting and debugging included, and after roughly two months they were running campaigns on their own. The idea: learn how the system should behave, then how we've actually modelled it, so they can design test cases on their own. One junior I mentored went further and learned AI-assisted scripting and context engineering while we built our nightly bench-health automation.
STAR-19 · Daimler

Pushed back on a management-directed HIL upgrade, with data

Our integration HILs in India, Germany and the US are meant to be exact replicas, because they're complex to maintain. Germany upgraded theirs for their vehicle variant, and management asked India to follow. But we were on a different variant, and upgrading mid-stream would have disrupted our daily testing. I analysed the delta between the two programmes and what each needed to test, and recommended we not upgrade yet, with the data to back it. My manager was sceptical at first, but once he saw the data he agreed. We pushed the upgrade from March to June, and in between my team kept the India HIL running and told the forums exactly what it could and couldn't test.
STAR-20 · Daimler, Sr. Engineer, 2020–2023

Cluster HMI tell-tale verification under COVID constraints: camera-based detection tool

During COVID, lab access was restricted and hardware was scarce, but cluster tell-tales still had to be verified. The team built an in-house camera-based detection tool, and I was responsible for the verification side: making sure that every time a new tell-tale came on the cluster, the camera captured it, the image was saved as evidence against that test step in PROVEtec, and it was compared against a reference image to pass or fail the step. So nobody had to be in the lab watching the cluster. A ready-made NI Vision system would have taken about eight months to arrive, so building our own saved that time and money.
STAR-21 · Daimler, current role, 2026

Nightly HIL health & inventory run: PowerShell → PROVEtec → Jenkins at 4 a.m.

With a junior engineer I mentored, I automated the health check of about nine HIL benches across India, the US and Germany. A PowerShell script drove PROVEtec to read each ECU's hardware, software and A-part numbers and run a power-on, driving and charging smoke test. Jenkins ran it on all the benches in parallel at 4 a.m. India time, and it took about half an hour. Before, the manual check took about two hours per bench, so it was done rarely and at random; the real gain was that it now ran every day, automatically, so faults were caught before the working day.
