Concept glossary

Term What it actually means
HIL (Hardware-in-the-Loop) Take the real electronic control unit (ECU) and connect it to a machine that pretends to be the rest of the vehicle (simulated engine, sensors, road, weather) in real time. The ECU thinks it's in a real vehicle; you can inject any condition (sensor failure, extreme temperature, GNSS loss) without touching a real vehicle.
SIL (Software-in-the-Loop) Same idea as HIL, but nothing is real hardware — both controller and environment are software models on a regular computer, usually earlier in development before physical hardware exists.
GNSS / RTK GNSS (e.g. GPS) gives rough position, usually accurate to a few metres. RTK (Real-Time Kinematic) uses a fixed reference station's corrections to improve that to centimetres.
Radar Sends radio waves, measures what bounces back — distance, speed, rough shape. Works in fog/dust/rain where cameras struggle, since radio waves aren't blocked by small particles the way light is.
Camera-based perception Image-processing/AI models on video frames to detect and classify objects. Strong at classification, weak in fog/dust/low light — hence pairing with radar and GNSS ("sensor fusion": each sensor's weakness covered by another's strength).
IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) Measures acceleration and rotation rate. Tracks vehicle motion moment-to-moment when GNSS is briefly lost — "dead reckoning."
Edge Compute An on-vehicle computer (not cloud) that processes sensor data and decides locally in real time, since there's no time to round-trip to a data centre while moving.
CAN bus (Controller Area Network) The standard in-vehicle wiring/protocol for ECUs to talk to each other.
ASIL / Safe State / Fault Reaction Time (ISO 26262) ASIL = risk classification for how dangerous a failure would be. Safe state = the defined, non-dangerous fallback condition. Fault reaction time = the max allowed time from detecting a problem to reaching safe state. Safety systems are built around "what happens, precisely and within what time, when it doesn't work" — not just "does it work."
ASPICE Formal maturity model for automotive software/systems engineering — names specific process areas (e.g. SYS.4 System Integration Test, SYS.5 System Qualification Test, SWE.5/SWE.6 software integration/qualification in PAM 3.1; older 2.5 names were ENG.x, see the version map) and requires specific documented work products for each.
ISTQB test-design techniques Formal vocabulary for maximum test coverage with minimum redundancy — equivalence partitioning (test one representative per group of similar inputs) and boundary value analysis (test at the edges of valid ranges, where bugs cluster).
SAT / UAT (Site/User Acceptance Test) The final test run with the customer watching/signing off — proves the system meets what was promised, vs. internal testing which only satisfies your own team.
RCA (Root-Cause Analysis) Tracing a failure to its actual underlying cause (not just the symptom) so the fix prevents recurrence.
Traceability matrix A table/link chain: requirement → test(s) that verify it → result/evidence. Proves nothing was missed.
Real-time system / latency Correctness depends on when the output arrives, not just what it is. A correct answer that's too late has still failed.
Ground loop Two or more ground paths at slightly different potentials, so current flows through ground/return conductors that should carry none. It shifts reference voltages and injects noise. In a HIL lab, high drive or load currents can make it bad enough to disturb the real-time system and I/O: the root cause of my STAR-02 (model reloads on high-torque steps). On my 5-cabinet SCALEXIO in 2025 it hit whenever a high-torque step demanded high current; dSPACE and the central hardware team fixed it around one common ground point. Typical fixes: single-point (star) grounding, isolating equipment grounds, and checking lab earthing with the facilities/hardware team.
Welded contactor An HV contactor whose contacts have fused shut, usually from arcing when switching under load or inrush current. It can no longer disconnect the traction battery, so HV stays live on the vehicle side even when commanded open: a serious safety fault. Detected by the missing "click" when commanded, by pre-charge/voltage checks across the contactor, or by the BMS's weld-detection diagnostic. I caught these by ear during Tata bring-up .
HV isolation & chassis bonding An EV's HV battery is floating: neither HV+ nor HV− is connected to the chassis (an IT-style system), so one insulation fault causes no shock current. What is connected to the chassis: (1) equipotential bonding: every HV component's metal housing (battery pack, inverter, motor, charger, DC-DC, compressor) is strapped to the chassis at low resistance (UNECE R100: < 0.1 Ω), so all touchable metal sits at one potential; (2) the insulation monitoring device (in the BMS) measures resistance HV+→chassis and HV−→chassis and warns or blocks contactor closing below the limit (R100: ≥ 100 Ω/V DC, ≥ 500 Ω/V AC); (3) Y-capacitors HV→chassis for EMC filtering. The 12 V system does use the chassis as its return. Why: a first fault is harmless and detected; a person can only be hurt if two faults happen, and bonding means even then they can't bridge two different potentials. my STAR-12 safety step. Analogies + fault walk-through: see notes.
A-part number / variant management At Daimler, the A-part number identifies a specific hardware or software variant; it's how variants are managed. Reading each ECU's A-part numbers tells you exactly which hardware and software variant a bench is running, which is what STAR-21's nightly run recorded. Elsewhere this is usually just called the ECU's hardware/software part number or variant coding.
12 V vs 24 V electrical systems The low-voltage supply that powers a vehicle's ECUs, lights and sensors. Passenger cars and most North American heavy trucks (e.g. Freightliner) use 12 V; European heavy trucks (e.g. Mercedes-Benz Actros) and most off-highway machines, including mining haul trucks, use 24 V. An ECU is designed for one of them, so a HIL's power-supply settings, power-up behaviour and under/over-voltage tests differ between the two. ISO 16750-2 defines the supply ranges and electrical tests for each separately. Bridge : in STAR-21 the US benches' ECUs ran at 12 V and the German benches' at 24 V, so their framework scripts differed.
MUX (multiplexed) switches Switches that send their state as data to a controller, instead of each switch having its own wire to an ECU input pin. Two kinds: (1) Bus-multiplexed: the switch panel has a small chip, usually a LIN slave (sometimes CAN), and reports every switch in one message; the controller can send back commands such as indicator LEDs and backlight dimming. (2) Resistor-coded: several switches or positions share one wire, each giving a different resistance, and the ECU reads the voltage to tell which is pressed (common for steering-wheel buttons and multi-position light switches). Truck examples: a dashboard panel of rocker switches (work lamps, beacon, fog lamps, differential lock, PTO) sent as one LIN frame to the body controller; a door switch pack (window up/down, mirror adjust, lock) sent to the door control module; the steering-column stalks (indicators, wipers, high beam) read by the steering column module and passed on over CAN. Why: far fewer wires (less weight and cost), switch faults show up in diagnostics, and software decides what each switch does, so a customer-specific switch can be added in a spare panel slot without a new harness. On a HIL: use the real panels (a person or a relay presses them) or let the restbus send the message the panel would send, so tests can "press" switches automatically. Test chain: switch pressed → message on the bus → body ECU decides → output drives the load (e.g. a lamp on the light wagon) → the HIL measures the lamp current. STAR-01's third cabinet has real MUX switches and switch panels . Whether they used LIN or CAN isn't confirmed.
FPGA board (in a HIL) A board whose logic runs as hardware, so a model can step in microseconds instead of a processor model's usual ~1 ms. E-mobility HILs use it to simulate the inverter and e-motor: the inverter's PWM switches tens of thousands of times a second, too fast for the processor model, and the ECU expects motor currents and position signals that react within microseconds. dSPACE offers FPGA boards for SCALEXIO, with ready-made electric-motor models. my ₹7–8 Cr SCALEXIO has FPGA cards that simulate inverter-motor operation .
Anti-jerk calibration A drivability calibration: tuning how motor torque ramps in and out (tip-in, tip-out, forward/reverse changes) so driveline backlash and shaft wind-up don't cause a shunt or oscillation the driver feels. In an EV the motor's instant torque makes this more noticeable than in a petrol car. I calibrated creep through anti-jerk on Tigor EV prototypes .
Brake-resistor chopper In an electric vehicle, braking energy normally goes back into the battery (regeneration). When the battery can't take it (full, or cold), a chopper, a fast power-electronic switch, sends the excess into a brake resistor, where it becomes heat, so electric braking keeps working. The chopper's ECU controls that switching. my SCALEXIO HV cabinet has two brake-resistor choppers .
Charging ECU An ECU that manages charging: it talks to the charging station and controls the vehicle's charging hardware. On my SCALEXIO the charging ECUs are three EV charge controllers; the two DC charge boxes and the megawatt charging unit they control are hardware, not ECUs . the DCCBs and the MWCU are hardware only; they're in the rig's count of 18 units, not among the 15 ECUs with logic.
DICV (Daimler India Commercial Vehicles) The name . STAR-01's restored rig serves DICV today . Daimler Truck's Indian subsidiary: it builds BharatBenz trucks and buses (plus export trucks) at Oragadam, near Chennai. Not the same company as my employer, Daimler Truck Innovation Center India in Bangalore. One of the two reasons for STAR-01's 2022 restoration: DICV needed HILs but couldn't spend a lot, and management was looking for low-cost solutions .
EVCC (Electric Vehicle Communication Controller) The vehicle's side of the charging conversation; my team calls it the electric vehicle charge controller. It talks to the charger's SECC (Supply Equipment Communication Controller) under DIN 70121 or ISO 15118, and commands the vehicle's charging hardware. Three on my SCALEXIO, controlling the DC charge boxes and the megawatt charging unit (over CAN FD), one EVCC each for the left CCS2 port, the right CCS2 port and the MCS port; the CCS2 ports' EVCCs handle AC sessions too .
HIL box How units that are high voltage on the vehicle sit on my HIL: at low voltage, as HIL boxes; that includes the inverters (PTIs), brake-resistor choppers (BRCs), DC charge boxes and the megawatt charging unit. Typically the real control electronics without the HV power stage, which is simulated instead (for an inverter, on an FPGA board).
DS2680 I/O Unit (dSPACE) A SCALEXIO "MultiCompact" I/O unit: one box with most of the I/O a powertrain or vehicle-dynamics ECU needs, plus ECU power-supply control, six power switches and a failure insertion unit, on IOCNET. On my SCALEXIO it controls the ECU supply voltage .
DS5366 Smart Charging Interface (dSPACE) An isolated interface between the HIL simulator and the charging ECU. It connects to the simulator over CAN FD, with USB and Ethernet for protocol tracing and logging, and ships with a Simulink model, ControlDesk layouts and a DBC. It simulates a charging station (or a vehicle) under ISO 15118, DIN 70121, CHAdeMO and GB/T, for Type 1, Type 2, CCS1, CCS2, GB/T and CHAdeMO plugs, including communication errors and Plug & Charge (TLS, certificates). DS5366_ADVANCED is the successor: an updated controller with stronger TLS crypto, and from dSPACE Release 2025-A the only one getting new ISO 15118-20 features; the original reaches end of life on 1 June 2028. my SCALEXIO has the DS5366 Advanced, with megawatt charging built in, and I configured it on the HIL with the dSPACE India and dSPACE Germany teams and the German HIL team, as a group exercise .
MCS (Megawatt Charging System) CharIN's DC charging standard for heavy-duty trucks and buses: up to 3.75 MW (3,000 A at 1,250 V DC), liquid-cooled connector (IEC 63379). The truck's EVCC talks to the charger's SECC over 10BASE-T1S single-pair automotive Ethernet with ISO 15118-20, instead of CCS's powerline. On my rig the megawatt charging unit is the vehicle's MCS charging hardware: like a DC charge box, but for megawatt charging, no logic of its own, and it talks to its EVCC over CAN FD . * the Ethernet link is EVCC ↔ charger, not the megawatt unit's.*
DC charge box Charging hardware with no logic of its own, controlled by an EV charge controller; the megawatt charging unit plays the same role for megawatt charging. Typically it holds the DC charging path between the inlet and the battery: contactors, fuses, voltage and current measurement. Two on my SCALEXIO, one per CCS2 port, left and right ; listed as DCCB among the rig's 18 units, hardware only .
CCS (Combined Charging System) The plug and protocol standard for charging cars and trucks in Europe and North America. "Combined" because one inlet takes both AC and DC. Messages run as powerline communication over the control-pilot wire.
CCS2 (CCS Combo 2) The CCS plug used in Europe and India: the Type 2 AC plug with two DC pins added below it. (CCS1 is the North American version, built on the Type 1 plug.) my SCALEXIO has a left and a right CCS2 port, each with its own EV charge controller and DC charge box; the trucks charge on AC through them too .
TLS (Transport Layer Security) The protocol that encrypts and authenticates a network connection: the "S" in HTTPS. ISO 15118 uses it to protect the charging messages and to carry the certificates for Plug & Charge; DIN 70121 has none.
10BASE-T1S Single-pair automotive Ethernet at 10 Mbit/s (IEEE 802.3cg) that can run as a multidrop bus like CAN, with PLCA taking turns so timing is deterministic. MCS uses it for the charger conversation.
Half duplex / full duplex Half duplex: data flows either way, but one direction at a time, like a walkie-talkie. CAN, CAN FD, LIN, 2-wire RS-485 and 10BASE-T1S are half duplex: all nodes share one pair, so they need a rule for who talks next. Full duplex: both ways at once, like a phone call: RS-232, 100BASE-T1, switched Ethernet.
Type 2 (IEC 62196-2) The standard AC charging plug in Europe, also used in India: three phases, neutral, earth, control pilot and proximity pilot. CCS2 is Type 2 plus two DC pins.
SECC (Supply Equipment Communication Controller) The charging station's communication controller, the counterpart of the vehicle's EVCC. (The station itself is the EVSE: Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment.)
EAM (Extended Activity Module) ECU The ECU that supports special lighting or other body requirements from a particular customer: the customer-specific extras on top of the BCM's standard body functions. It also handles drive authorization; a programmable node (ECU) in commercial heavy-duty trucks. On my restored PHS rig it sits in the body-domain rack and was the first ECU brought up (STAR-01). Drive authorization is the immobiliser function: the vehicle starts and drives only after the key or driver ID is verified (Mercedes-Benz's term is "drive authorization system"). Background on the name, not your truck: in Mercedes-Benz service notes for the 2000–05 ML-Class, the Extended Activity Module is a second module next to the main body controller (the All Activity Module), linked over CAN; "extended" means it adds functions to the main body controller, which fits my description.

Protocol primer — deep dive + comparative table

Layman explanations

UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter) — Two wires (TX/RX) + ground. No clock wire; both sides agree a baud rate in advance and time their own bits against it ("asynchronous"). Chip-level voltages (0–3.3V/0–5V, "TTL"). Point-to-point only, no addressing.

RS-232 — Same async framing as UART, but standardized to much larger voltages (~±3V to ±15V) so signals survive longer/noisier cables. Needs a converter IC (classically MAX232) between chip-level UART and cable-level RS-232. Historically DB9/DB25 connectors. Point-to-point, up to ~15 m practical range.

NMEA 0183 — Not a wire protocol, a message format carried over serial. Comma-separated ASCII "sentences" starting with $ (e.g. $GPGGA,...), checksum at the end. Historically 4800 baud; modern receivers usually faster and configurable. One-directional (receiver talks, consumer listens).

CAN (Controller Area Network) — Two wires (CAN-H/CAN-L), differential signal (noise-resistant). Every node sees every message; no addressing, just message IDs. Simultaneous transmission resolves by ID priority (lower ID wins — "arbitration"), no traffic cop needed. Classic CAN: up to 1 Mbit/s, 8 bytes/message.

CAN-FD (Flexible Data-Rate) — Same wires/arbitration as CAN, but after arbitration wins, switches to a faster data phase (commonly 5–8 Mbit/s) and up to 64 bytes/message.

Automotive Ethernet — Point-to-point, packet-switched, single twisted pair (100BASE-T1 = 100 Mbit/s, 1000BASE-T1 = 1 Gbit/s) vs. regular Ethernet's 4 pairs. Needs switches for >2 devices; addressable traffic. Far higher bandwidth than CAN — carries video, dense sensor fusion, diagnostics. ~15 m per segment, extended via switches.

SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface) — Synchronous (shared clock, SCLK), full-duplex, 4 wires (SCLK/MOSI/MISO/CS per device). Fast (tens of MHz). Board-level distance only.

I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit) — Synchronous, 2 wires total (SCL clock + shared SDA data), many devices via 7-bit addressing (up to 128). Slower than SPI (100/400 kHz typical). Board-level distance.

Other protocols referenced elsewhere in this bank

Protocol Explanation
LIN (Local Interconnect Network) Cheap single-wire cousin of CAN, up to 19.2 kbit/s, one master + several slaves. Non-safety-critical, low-priority signals.
GMSL / FPD-Link (SerDes) Proprietary high-speed point-to-point video links (Analog Devices/Maxim GMSL, TI FPD-Link) — raw uncompressed camera video + power + control down a single coax/twisted pair over several metres ("Power-over-Coax").
RTCM3 Binary message format for GNSS correction data.
NTRIP "Networked Transport of RTCM via Internet Protocol" — RTCM3 streamed over cellular/Wi-Fi from a base-station network to a rover.
XCP / CCP XCP = Universal Measurement and Calibration Protocol; CCP = its older, CAN-only predecessor (CAN Calibration Protocol). Both let tools (INCA, CANape) read/write ECU calibration parameters and watch internal variables live without stopping/reflashing the ECU. XCP runs over CAN, CAN-FD, Ethernet, SPI, or serial; CCP was CAN-only.

Block What it is
ECU The one genuinely real hardware component — everything below exists to make it believe it's in a real vehicle.
Wiring Harness The real cable bundle connecting the ECU's connector to the lab setup.
Hypertac Plug/Socket High-density, gold-plated, spring-pin ("pogo pin") connectors built for very frequent connect/disconnect cycles without wear — what a constantly-replugged lab bench needs.
FIU / BOB FIU = Failure Insertion Unit — switching matrix that deliberately breaks/shorts-to-ground/shorts-to-battery a signal on command, for wiring-fault simulation without cutting a wire. BOB = Break-Out Box — every harness pin broken out to an accessible test point.
IO Cards (ADC/DAC) ADC reads real analog ECU signals into digital values; DAC turns simulation output back into a real analog voltage the ECU reads as if from a real sensor.
Load Cards (FPGA) Realistic electrical loads so a simulated motor/solenoid "feels" electrically real to the ECU, not just logically correct. FPGA-based for hardware-speed response.
SC Cards (ADC/DAC) Most likely Signal Conditioning cards (scale/filter/amplify/protect signals between IO layer and the rest) — confirm this exact expansion against lab notes if it comes up; transcribed faithfully, not guessed at dSPACE's own internal naming.
Backplane Interconnect The internal bus all cards (IO, Load, SC, Processor) plug into — power + high-speed data paths, why they're not separately wired point-to-point.
Processor (dig) The real-time digital core — where the plant model (simulated vehicle/environment/physics) actually runs.
IOCNET Router The high-speed network connecting the processor unit to distributed IO units/cabinets — dSPACE's real-time IO network in the SCALEXIO ecosystem, distinct from the vehicle-facing CAN/Ethernet in .

Why this matters generally: this is the physical answer to "how does a sensor's electrical signal get from the real world into a simulation and back" — the mechanical foundation underneath calibration discussion. Fault injection happens at the FIU. The sensor's raw-value ↔ physical-value calibration parameter lives at the ADC/DAC conversion (IO/SC Cards). Load Cards are why a simulated actuator feels electrically real, not just logically correct.

Creating and sending a CAN message

What you're building

A CAN frame is an ID (11-bit standard or 29-bit extended; the lower ID wins arbitration), a DLC (0–8 data bytes on classic CAN) and the data. Signals are packed into the data bytes at a start bit and length. A factor and offset convert the physical value to the raw value, and the byte order is Intel (little-endian) or Motorola (big-endian). All of this lives in the communication matrix: a DBC file for CAN (ARXML in AUTOSAR projects, LDF for LIN). The matrix also sets the send rule: cyclic (e.g. every 100 ms), on change, or on request.

Worked example (the frame in the Python test below): ID 0x321, 8 bytes, every 100 ms. WorkLampReq is bit 0; DimLevel is byte 1, with a factor of 0.5 %. Setting WorkLampReq = 1 and DimLevel = 75 % gives raw 75 / 0.5 = 150 = 0x96, so with AliveCounter = 3 in the last byte the data is 01 96 00 00 00 00 00 03.

Before anything goes on the wire (every method)

  • A CAN channel and a bus with 120 Ω at each end (60 Ω between CAN_H and CAN_L), and at least one other active node to acknowledge: an ECU, a second channel or an analyser .
  • The same bit rate as the bus, e.g. 250 or 500 kbit/s.
  • Never send an ID that another node on a live network already sends: two senders of one ID corrupt each other's frames.

A. On a dSPACE SCALEXIO: ConfigurationDesk Bus Manager → ControlDesk

  1. Import the communication matrix into the Bus Manager (DBC, LDF, FIBEX or AUTOSAR files).
  2. Build a bus configuration: drag the frame, or its whole sending ECU, into the bus configuration as a simulated ECU, so the HIL transmits it (restbus).
  3. Set how it's sent: the cycle time (from the DBC or overridden), which signals get model access (driven from Simulink) and which you'll set by hand. If the frame is E2E-protected, add the counter and CRC handling .
  4. Map it to hardware: assign the bus configuration's CAN channel to a physical CAN channel of the SCALEXIO I/O, and check the bit rate.
  5. Build the real-time application and download it to the SCALEXIO.
  6. In ControlDesk, open the Bus Navigator, find the frame and set its signal values. It goes out on the physical bus at its cycle time; the Bus Navigator's monitor shows the traffic.

C. Python: python-can + cantools ( tested)

Two bus objects on the same virtual channel behave like two HIL channels wired together, so this needs no hardware. Output of the run: single frame: 0x321 01 96 00 00 00 00 00 03 and periodic: 6 frames in 0.55 s at 100 ms.

"""Create a CAN message from a DBC definition and send it on a CAN bus.

Runs on python-can's virtual bus (no hardware): two bus objects on the same
channel act like two HIL CAN channels wired together (a loopback).
For a real bus, swap the virtual Bus lines for a hardware interface, e.g.
    can.Bus(interface="pcan", channel="PCAN_USBBUS1", bitrate=500000)
    can.Bus(interface="vector", channel=0, bitrate=500000)
"""
import time

import can
import cantools

# 1. The definition: one frame, ID 0x321 (801), 8 bytes, sent by the HIL every 100 ms.
DBC = """VERSION ""

NS_ :

BS_:

BU_: HIL EAM

BO_ 801 LightRequest: 8 HIL
 SG_ WorkLampReq : 0|1@1+ (1,0) [0|1] "" EAM
 SG_ BeaconReq : 1|1@1+ (1,0) [0|1] "" EAM
 SG_ DimLevel : 8|8@1+ (0.5,0) [0|100] "%" EAM
 SG_ AliveCounter : 56|4@1+ (1,0) [0|15] "" EAM

BA_DEF_ BO_ "GenMsgCycleTime" INT 0 10000;
BA_DEF_DEF_ "GenMsgCycleTime" 0;
BA_ "GenMsgCycleTime" BO_ 801 100;
"""

db = cantools.database.load_string(DBC, database_format="dbc")
light_req = db.get_message_by_name("LightRequest")

# 2. Encode physical values into the 8 data bytes (factor/offset and bit positions from the DBC).
payload = light_req.encode({"WorkLampReq": 1, "BeaconReq": 0, "DimLevel": 75.0, "AliveCounter": 3})
assert payload == bytes([0x01, 0x96, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0x03]), payload.hex(" ") # 75 % / 0.5 = 150 = 0x96

# 3. Wrap it in a CAN frame.
frame = can.Message(arbitration_id=light_req.frame_id, data=payload, is_extended_id=light_req.is_extended_frame)

with can.Bus(interface="virtual", channel="hil") as tx, can.Bus(interface="virtual", channel="hil") as rx:
    # 4a. Send once; the second "channel" must receive it byte for byte.
    tx.send(frame)
    got = rx.recv(timeout=1.0)
    assert got is not None, "nothing received"
    assert got.arbitration_id == 0x321 and got.dlc == 8 and bytes(got.data) == payload
    print("single frame:", hex(got.arbitration_id), got.data.hex(" "), light_req.decode(got.data))

    # 4b. Send cyclically at the DBC's cycle time, as an ECU or restbus would.
    period_s = light_req.cycle_time / 1000
    task = tx.send_periodic(frame, period=period_s)
    time.sleep(0.55)
    task.stop
    received = 0
    while rx.recv(timeout=0.05) is not None:
        received += 1
    assert 4 <= received <= 7, received # ~0.55 s at 100 ms
    print(f"periodic: {received} frames in 0.55 s at {light_req.cycle_time} ms")

AUTOSAR E2E Profile 2 vs Profile 5

What every profile does: the sender adds a CRC and a counter. The data ID, unique to each message, goes into the CRC but is never sent. The receiver recomputes the CRC with the data ID it expects, then checks how far the counter stepped.

Fault Caught by
Corrupted data CRC
Repeated / frozen message The counter didn't change
Lost messages The counter jumped more than the allowed step
Wrong order The counter went backwards
Masquerading (the right bytes, but the wrong message or sender) The data ID inside the CRC
The message stopped Not in the frame: the receiver's timeout
Profile 2 Profile 5
For Classic CAN (≤ 8 bytes) Larger frames, typically CAN FD
CRC 8-bit (CRC-8, polynomial 0x2F, "CRC8H2F") 16-bit (CRC-16 CCITT-FALSE: polynomial 0x1021, init 0xFFFF)
Counter 4-bit (0–15) 8-bit (0–255)
Data ID A list of 16; the counter value picks which one goes into the CRC One 16-bit data ID
Overhead 2 bytes 3 bytes

What the differences mean: - A 16-bit CRC misses far fewer corruptions, which matters because a CAN FD frame carries more data that can be corrupted. - A 4-bit counter wraps after 16 frames, so losing exactly 16 frames can go unseen. - Profile 2 fits into 2 bytes because a classic CAN frame only has 8.

Receiver status → state machine: each check returns a status: OK, NO_NEW_DATA, WRONG_CRC, REPEATED, OK_SOME_LOST or WRONG_SEQUENCE. A windowed state machine (NODATA → INIT → VALID ⇄ INVALID) judges the last N results, so scattered errors add up. A "3 bad in a row" count never sees errors separated by good frames; the POC's mutation tests catch exactly that bug. It's the same debounce trade-off as in see notes.

E2E protection AUTOSAR end-to-end protection: a CRC, counter and data ID added by the sender and checked by the receiver. Corruption, repetition, loss, reordering and masquerading are all detected end to end, across every gateway in between
Data ID A number unique to each protected message, included in the CRC but never transmitted. A correct-looking frame from the wrong message fails the CRC
E2E state machine A windowed judgement of the last N check results (valid / invalid): one bad frame doesn't trip it, but scattered errors do

CI on a HIL bench: how a physical bench becomes a "sandbox"

Why this section exists: question bank G-07 ("Practical limits of CI on HIL?"), and my own question: CI runs in an isolated, throwaway sandbox, so how can a physical HIL bench be one?

The mismatch

Cloud CI sandbox HIL bench
Disposable: a fresh container per job, destroyed afterwards Physical and expensive: a handful of benches, shared by several teams
Identical every time, because it's built from an image Stateful: it remembers the flashed software, fault memory (DTCs), NVM, FIU relay positions and the loaded plant model
Scales instantly: 50 jobs = 50 containers Doesn't scale: one pipeline per bench at a time
Minutes per job A full regression can take hours

The answer: isolation in time, not in space

You don't sandbox the bench. You give each run the bench to itself, and make every run start clean:

  1. A CI agent on the bench PC. A Jenkins agent, GitHub Actions self-hosted runner or GitLab runner on the PC wired to the HIL. Labels (bench, platform, ECU variant) route each job to the right bench. STAR-21: one Jenkins agent on each bench PC ran all ~9 benches in parallel.
  2. An exclusive lock. One pipeline owns the bench; the others queue. (Jenkins: a single executor, or the Lockable Resources plugin's lock. GitHub Actions: a concurrency group. GitLab: resource_group.) STAR-21 took the bench by schedule instead: at 4 a.m. India time it closed any open PROVEtec session and restarted it.
  3. Reset to a known state: this is the sandbox. Every run starts the same way:
  4. restart the test tool, so no stale session or settings carry over ( STAR-21's job closed and restarted PROVEtec before every run, even if someone was on the bench)
  5. power-cycle the ECU from a programmable supply (often controlled over RS-232/LAN: )
  6. flash the exact build the pipeline just produced (UDS programming session + download: see notes)
  7. clear the fault memory and reset the ECU (UDS 0x14, 0x11)
  8. load the pinned plant model, ConfigurationDesk project and restbus configuration, and record their versions
  9. put the FIU back to no-fault
  10. run a health check / smoke before any real test: bench alive, communication up, no stray DTCs
  11. Run, then tear down. Run the tests, publish results tied to requirement IDs, leave the bench in a safe idle state, release the lock.
  12. Version everything. Test scripts, framework libraries, plant model and bench configuration are tagged with the software build under test (G-09), so any run can be reproduced.

A tiered pipeline: not every commit touches the bench

Every commit → build + static analysis + unit tests + SIL (cloud containers: true sandboxes)
Every build/merge → flash to HIL → health check → smoke set (minutes)
Nightly → full HIL regression (hours)
Release → release-gate suite + trace report → human sign-off

Fail policy: infrastructure vs product

If flashing or the health check fails, the run is infrastructure / blocked: retry once, or take the bench out of the pool and alert its owner. Don't report it as a product bug. Only a failure after a clean health check counts against the software. (Same split as see notes three suspects; flaky tests: .)

Your real version (STAR-21): when the nightly check failed a bench, the HIL operators booked it, root-caused it, and told testers in advance about the issue, its dependencies and the expected fix date, before anyone had to escalate.

"Ready for deployment" means something different here

In automotive, a green pipeline doesn't deploy anything to vehicles. It produces a release candidate plus evidence (reports tied to requirements), and a human signs off the release gate.

The HIL bench is the staging environment of an ECU pipeline, not its sandbox. You've already designed that pattern once.

The practical limits (G-07)

  • Scarce benches: HIL can't run on every commit at cloud scale → tiering, nightly runs, a bench pool.
  • State: a run is only as clean as its reset.
  • Sharing: queues, so bench scheduling matters. STAR-08: you replaced ad hoc booking with self-service scheduling (20–22 h/day utilisation). That was booking by people; the CI lock is the automated version.
  • Speed: a smoke subset per build, the full regression nightly.
  • Hardware drift (relays, connectors, grounding): health checks and bench KPIs catch it. STAR-02's ground loop is why that matters.
  • The physical wiring can't be versioned: record the bench ID with every result.

Your Daimler setup ( STAR-21) was scheduled continuous testing: a nightly 4 a.m. Jenkins job for bench health and inventory across ~9 benches in India, the USA and Germany, run on all of them in parallel in about half an hour. 4 a.m. India time, for every bench, was chosen because most testers were in India, so the benches were least used then: isolation in time, done by scheduling. Result: the check ran every day, automatically, instead of by hand, and faults were caught before the working day.

Oscilloscope settings

Rail check (step 2) - Ripple and noise: AC coupling (blocks the DC so you can zoom to millivolts), 20–50 mV/div; timebase around 10 µs/div for switching noise, 1 ms/div for slower ripple. Rule of thumb from the write-up: under ~50 mV peak-to-peak on the 5 V logic rail, under ~120 mV on 12/24 V. The real limit is what the boards tolerate (check the datasheets): a typical off-the-shelf 5 V supply may itself be specified above 50 mV.

I/O board check (step 4) - A diagnostic model toggles every digital output at 1 Hz: 500 ms high, 500 ms low. - DC coupling; 2 V/div for 5 V logic, 5 V/div for 12/24 V outputs. - Timebase 100–200 ms/div: ten divisions show one to two full periods. (The write-up's 500 ms/div shows five.) - Trigger: Normal, not Auto (Auto free-runs between slow edges, so the trace won't hold still); rising edge; level at half the expected high (2.5 V for 5 V logic). - Pass: square edges and a flat high level. Rounded edges or a drooping high point to a damaged output driver on that board.

What survives heavy transport (typical pattern, not a record of my rig)

  • Usually fine: backplanes, cabinet frames, passive load-resistor banks, and processor boards whose card guides stayed locked.
  • High risk: mechanical relays in the FIU (stuck contacts, dislodged springs); heavy harnesses without strain relief pulling their own pins out; hairline PCB cracks near heavy components such as large capacitors.

Replacing damaged FIU channels with external relays

Mechanical relays give a true open circuit. Solid-state relays leak a small current when "off" and add on-resistance, which can hide an open-load fault from the ECU's diagnostics or shift a measured current. Keep open-circuit faults on mechanical contacts, and document every rerouted channel so each test result stays traceable to the rig configuration it ran on.

CAN loopback, step by step

  • General HIL practice, not a record of what the STAR-01 team did. Whether their loopback covered the CAN channels isn't confirmed. *

The idea: a CAN frame only counts as sent when another node acknowledges it. So one channel can't test itself. Wire two channels of the HIL's CAN board together, make one the sender and the other the receiver, check every frame arrives intact, then swap roles.

  1. Wire: CAN_H to CAN_H, CAN_L to CAN_L, ground to ground. On a 9-pin D-sub (CiA 303-1): pin 7 = CAN_H, pin 2 = CAN_L, pin 3 = ground. Swapping H and L gives only errors, no valid frames.
  2. Terminate: a CAN bus needs 120 Ω at each end. With power off, measure between CAN_H and CAN_L: 60 Ω = both ends terminated (correct) · 120 Ω = one end only · open = none · near 0 Ω = a short. A short test cable often works without termination, which hides the problem until the real harness goes on.
  3. Configure both channels the same: the same bit rate (e.g. 250 or 500 kbit/s) and the same transceiver type. The DS4302 offers high-speed and fault-tolerant low-speed CAN, and the two can't share wires. The receiver must be active, not in listen-only mode, or nothing sends the acknowledgement. For CAN FD: both channels need the same arbitration and data bit rates, and FD-capable transceivers; termination matters more at the faster data rate.
  4. Send and check: channel A sends a known frame every 10 ms, e.g. ID 0x123 with 8 bytes 01 02 03 04 05 06 07 08. Channel B checks the ID, the length (DLC) and every byte, and counts: 1,000 sent = 1,000 received. Try an 11-bit ID and a 29-bit (extended) one, since truck networks on J1939 use 29-bit IDs. Then reverse: B sends, A receives.
  5. Pass: every frame arrives with the right ID and data, no error frames, and both channels' error counters stay at 0.
Symptom Likely cause

On a scope (optional): high-speed CAN idles (recessive) with both lines at about 2.5 V. A dominant bit pulls CAN_H to about 3.5 V and CAN_L to about 1.5 V. Clean, square edges mean a healthy bus.

Why it matters on a HIL: it proves the CAN board, its transceivers, the harness to the breakout box and the bit-rate setting before an ECU is connected. If the ECU then stays silent, the fault is on the ECU side or in the restbus setup, not the rig. The same wiring can also provoke faults on purpose (short H to L through the FIU → bus-off), which is the bus-level fault injection in see notes.

Ground loop on an e-mobility HIL

What happened

The story, roles and wording: STAR-02. In short: 2025, the 5-cabinet SCALEXIO; the HIL reloaded its models on every high-torque, high-current step; 2–3 months on software; the SME pointed to grounding; dSPACE and the central hardware team re-grounded it around one common point.

How a ground loop does this, step by step

  1. Every current needs a way back. Whatever current a high-torque step draws flows out through the supply wires and back through ground (return) conductors.
  2. Ground wires aren't perfect. A strap or cable has a little resistance and inductance, so current through it makes a voltage across it: V = I × R for steady current, plus L × di/dt spikes whenever the current changes quickly. Generic illustration, not your rig: 20 A through 10 mΩ of strap is 0.2 V; a fast current step through a metre of cable (about 1 µH) adds spikes on top.
  3. A loop means more than one way back. If equipment is grounded at more than one point (each cabinet to its own earth, plus cable shields and signal grounds between cabinets), the return current splits across every path, including paths that were only meant to be a signal reference.
  4. So "0 V" stops being the same everywhere. During the high-current step, one cabinet's ground sits a little above or below another's, with spikes every time the current changes. Every signal and data link between those cabinets sees that difference as noise.
  5. Past the margin, the real-time system falls over. Electronics tolerate some ground noise. Beyond it, a processor resets or a real-time link drops; the real-time application stops and has to be loaded again: the "model reload". Which part reset on your rig isn't recorded, so don't name one.
  6. Why only on high-torque steps: the ground current grows with the load. At low load the shift stays inside the margin; a high-torque step pushes it over.
  7. Why it looked like software: the symptom is a software symptom (a model reload), and it came during particular test scenarios, which points people at the model or controller running them.

Why a multi-cabinet rig is prone to it

The fix: one common ground point

Star (single-point) grounding: every cabinet's ground goes by its own heavy strap to one point or busbar, and there are no other ground paths between cabinets. Then there's only one way back, every cabinet shares the same 0 V, and the load current returns along its own conductor instead of through the signal references. Often done alongside it (not claimed for your rig): cable shields bonded at one end, or 360° at both ends with a proper equipotential bond; isolated or fibre links for signals between cabinets; short, heavy bonding straps.

Equipotential bonding: why every metal part is tied together

The core idea: current only flows between two points at different voltages. If everything a person can touch is at the same voltage, nothing flows through their body, even if all of it is at hundreds of volts. Bonding doesn't remove the voltage; it removes the difference.

Analogies

Analogy How it maps
Bird on a power line Both feet at 11 kV → no difference → no current. A wing touching a pole (another potential) → current. Bonding makes the whole car one "wire".
Water tanks joined by a wide pipe Voltage = water level, current = flow. Wide pipes (bonding straps) keep every tank at one level. A thin or corroded strap = a clogged pipe: levels drift apart, and that's the danger.
Two people in a lift They can shake hands on floor 10 or floor 0; only the difference between them matters, not the height above ground.

Everyday examples

  • Swimming pools: ladder, handrails, pump housing and the concrete's steel are bonded, so a pump fault doesn't put a voltage between the water and the rail.
  • Bathrooms (UK wiring practice): metal taps, pipes and the bath were traditionally bonded so wet skin can't bridge two potentials.
  • Operating theatres: all metal near the patient is bonded; even small differences are dangerous with internal probes.
  • Aircraft refuelling: tanker and aircraft are bonded before fuelling so no spark jumps between them.

In an EV: one fault, walked through

Setup: 350 V floating battery; inverter and motor housings bonded to the chassis at < 0.1 Ω.

  1. Fault: a chafed HV+ cable touches the inverter housing.
  2. With bonding: inverter housing, motor housing, body panels and door handles all shift together. Hand on the door + hand on the motor housing = 0 V across you.
  3. Why one fault is still safe: the battery floats; HV− isn't on the chassis, so there's no return path and almost no current. The insulation monitor sees HV+→chassis resistance collapse, flags it and blocks contactor closing.
  4. Without bonding: the inverter housing is live, the panel next to it isn't, and a technician touching both becomes the connection.

Why the strap must be low-resistance: voltage across the strap = fault current × resistance. 10 A × 0.1 Ω = 1 V (harmless); a corroded 50 Ω strap → 500 V across the gap (lethal). Hence R100's < 0.1 Ω limit, checked with a low-resistance ohmmeter.

Why a DS2680 instead of separate boards? One compact unit with the I/O, power control, switching and fault insertion that a powertrain ECU needs: less wiring and fewer boards to configure. Separate HighFlex boards give more channels or special functions.
How does it talk to the processor? IOCNET, dSPACE's real-time I/O network, the same as every SCALEXIO I/O unit; it can reach units in other cabinets.

2. What only a real bus teaches (CAN error confinement)

Each CAN node keeps a transmit (TEC) and a receive (REC) error counter: error active < 128 ≤ error passive < 256 ≤ bus-off. - The ACK-error exception (ISO 11898-1): a transmitter that is error passive and sees no ACK doesn't keep incrementing its TEC. So on a two-node bus, pulling a wire leaves the sender error passive, not bus-off. A safety controller that only watches for bus-off misses it, so it should treat transmit-error-passive as an actuator-bus fault too. - The corollary: one board alone on a bus never gets an ACK, so it sits error passive permanently. Bus monitoring must be off for single-board tests (a bench bug caught on review, before any hardware run). - Short lab wires hide termination problems that a vehicle harness wouldn't. Common ground matters for the transceiver's common-mode range.

3. Porting a model to C++ bit-exactly: four rules (from the bench)

  1. Python's round rounds halves to even: use nearbyint, not lround.
  2. Python's % is never negative: emulate it for timestamps and counters.
  3. Keep Python's tie behaviour of max/min (the first argument wins on equality).
  4. No fused multiply-add: -ffp-contract=off.

Also: the ESP32-S3 FPU is single-precision, so double runs in software. That's exact, but slower: measure the worst-case execution time on the target, then decide whether float would change any verdict (back-to-back again).

4. PC-bench transport traps (bench v2.1, CAN between processes on Windows)

  • The bench must prove it keeps time (v2.3.1–2). A board with its own clock judges command age against it, so a PC that stalls makes fresh commands look stale, and the DUT reacts to the bench's fault. Measure the bench's lag on every real-time run and fail the run, not the DUT, above a few ms. Measured: 20–70 ms stalls on a laptop on battery (Balanced plan, CPU at base clock, other apps at 44% load), ≤ 2.6 ms on mains. Mitigations: mains power, a performance power plan, high process priority + a 1 ms timer (timeBeginPeriod), no heavy apps.

  • Dry-run against an emulated board first: the board's exact firmware logic in a separate process, reached like a COM port. It caught a pyserial socket:// read bug and the lag problem before any wiring. An emulator thread inside the bench runs late (Python's GIL), the way a slow MCU would, so it must be its own process.

  • UDP multicast delivered every frame twice, some 60–90 ms late. A duplicate looks like a REPEATED E2E frame, so it caused false stops. Fix: sender sequence IDs.

  • Windows sleep granularity (~10 ms) made a 10 ms controller run late: busy-wait or a real-time OS.
  • A late duplicate time message looked like a 16-bit clock wrap.
  • Zombie processes from killed runs injected stale frames: always close the DUT in a finally.
  • Opening a COM port can reset an ESP32 (DTR/RTS): hold both low and wait for the firmware's hello.

The layers, bottom to top

  1. Basic signalling (IEC 61851-1): the charger puts a 1 kHz PWM signal on the control pilot, and the vehicle changes its voltage level with a resistor. The level gives the state: A +12 V (nothing connected) · B +9 V (connected, not ready) · C +6 V (ready to charge) · D +3 V (charging, ventilation needed) · E 0 V (error) · F −12 V (charger fault). A 5 % duty cycle means "digital communication required"; DC charging always uses it.
  2. The link that carries the messages: CCS uses PLC over the control-pilot wire (ISO 15118-3). MCS uses 10BASE-T1S single-pair automotive Ethernet (ISO 15118-10).
  3. The application protocol: DIN SPEC 70121, ISO 15118-2 or ISO 15118-20. The very first message, supportedAppProtocol, lists the protocols the vehicle speaks, and the charger picks one.

10BASE-T1S in one page

  • IEEE 802.3cg-2019

The name, decoded: 10 = 10 Mbit/s · BASE = baseband · T1 = one twisted pair · S = short reach.

  • Speed and duplex: 10 Mbit/s, half duplex: one node talks at a time (see "Half duplex vs full duplex" below).
  • Topology: point-to-point, or a multidrop bus like CAN: at least 8 nodes on one pair, up to 25 m. That's the big difference from 100BASE-T1 and 1000BASE-T1, which are point-to-point only and need a switch.
  • Who talks when: classic Ethernet collision detection (CSMA/CD), or PLCA (Physical Layer Collision Avoidance): the nodes take turns in a fixed round-robin, so the worst-case delay is bounded. That makes it deterministic, like CAN.
  • Why vehicles use it: it's real Ethernet, so IP, TCP and TLS run on it unchanged, but on one cheap pair and as a bus. It's pitched as a replacement for CAN and LIN in zonal architectures, and it removes the gateway between CAN and Ethernet.
  • Why MCS uses it: the ISO 15118-20 conversation is IP-based and secured with TLS. 10BASE-T1S carries it over a wired Ethernet link (ISO 15118-10), instead of CCS's powerline signal riding on the control-pilot wire.

Half duplex vs full duplex

  • General. *

  • Full duplex: both sides send at the same time, like a phone call. Examples: RS-232 (separate transmit and receive wires), 100BASE-T1 and 1000BASE-T1 automotive Ethernet (both directions on one pair at once, using echo cancellation), switched Ethernet in general.

  • Half duplex: data can flow either way, but only one direction at a time, like a walkie-talkie. CAN, CAN FD, LIN, 2-wire RS-485 and 10BASE-T1S are all half duplex: every node shares the same wires, so only one can drive them at a time, and each needs a rule for who talks next. CAN uses arbitration by ID, LIN the master's schedule, 10BASE-T1S PLCA turns.
  • Simplex: one direction only, e.g. a sensor that only transmits.
  • What it means in practice: on a half-duplex bus the bandwidth is shared. 10BASE-T1S's 10 Mbit/s is split across every node on the bus, and a node waits its turn. That's why bus load and message priority matter on CAN, and why a timing guarantee (arbitration, PLCA) is the selling point of these buses.

A DC charging session, step by step (DIN 70121 / ISO 15118-2 message names)

  1. Plug in: PP detects the plug; the pilot goes to state B with a 5 % duty cycle.
  2. Link up: SLAC pairs the PLC modems (CCS); SDP finds the charger's address.
  3. supportedAppProtocol: agree on DIN 70121 or ISO 15118.
  4. SessionSetup → ServiceDiscovery → authorisation (external, or Plug & Charge).
  5. ChargeParameterDiscovery: both sides exchange limits: maximum voltage, current and power, and the battery's state.
  6. CableCheck: the charger tests the cable's insulation at voltage.
  7. PreCharge: the charger raises its output to the battery's voltage, so the contactors can close without a current surge.
  8. PowerDelivery (start) → CurrentDemand loop: the vehicle keeps requesting voltage and current; the charger reports what it's delivering.
  9. PowerDelivery (stop) → WeldingDetection (checks the contactors really opened) → SessionStop.

The vehicle's charging contactors switch at steps 7 and 9. On my rig, the EVCCs control the charging hardware , so that's where those commands would go.

Automotive radar (77 GHz FMCW) + radar vs ultrasonic

1. How FMCW radar works

FMCW = frequency-modulated continuous wave. The radar transmits a chirp: a signal whose frequency rises steadily, e.g. from 76 to 77 GHz in tens of microseconds. The echo is the same chirp, delayed by the round trip.

It measures How Rule of thumb
Range Mix the outgoing chirp with the echo. Because the frequency is ramping, the delayed echo is at a slightly lower frequency; the difference (the beat frequency) is proportional to distance. An FFT turns beat frequencies into range bins. Range resolution = c / (2 × bandwidth): 1 GHz → 15 cm; 4 GHz (77–81 GHz) → ~4 cm.
Relative speed A moving target shifts the echo's phase a little from chirp to chirp (Doppler). A second FFT across many chirps gives the speed. Result: a range-Doppler map. Speed comes directly, in one frame: radar's big advantage.
Angle Several receive antennas (often a MIMO "virtual array") hear the echo with slightly different phases; the phase difference gives the direction. Angular resolution is limited by antenna size: typically a few degrees. Radar's weak point vs camera or LiDAR.

From signal to objects: range-Doppler-angle data → CFAR detection (a threshold that adapts to the local noise, so the false-alarm rate stays constant) → clustering → tracking (usually a Kalman filter: confirm a target over several frames, predict it, drop it when it disappears) → an object list: range, speed, angle, RCS, existence probability.

RCS (radar cross-section): how "bright" a target looks to radar, in m². A truck is huge, a person is about 1 m², a corner reflector is built to a known RCS. Detection range depends on RCS: small targets are seen later.

Output to the ECU: an object list over CAN FD (the common case) or detections / a point cloud over automotive Ethernet (higher bandwidth). Messages usually carry end-to-end protection (CRC + alive counter) so the receiver can spot corrupted or frozen data.

2. Why radar is the fog sensor, and where it's weak

  • Fog, dust, darkness: a 77 GHz wave is ~4 mm long; fog droplets are micrometres. Particles much smaller than the wavelength barely scatter it. So radar sees through fog and dust where the camera loses contrast and LiDAR is scattered.
  • Weak points:
  • Poor angular resolution: two objects close together can merge; hard to say which lane a target is in far away.
  • Poor classification: it sees "something with this range, speed and RCS", not "a person" or "a rock".
  • Stationary objects: the world is full of stationary returns (walls, signs, berms, bridges). With weak elevation resolution a radar can't easily tell an overhead structure from a stopped vehicle, so many trackers de-prioritise stationary targets. That matters for static obstacles on a haul road: test it explicitly. Newer 4D imaging radars add elevation and help.
  • Heavy rain and wet snow attenuate somewhat; mud, ice or dust caked on the radome attenuates a lot.

Deeper : - Scattering vs size: particles much smaller than the wavelength barely scatter it (Rayleigh: roughly size⁶ / wavelength⁴); particles the size of the wavelength or bigger scatter strongly.

Wave Wavelength vs a fog droplet (~1–20 µm)
Visible light (camera) ~0.0005 mm droplet bigger → strong scattering → contrast lost
LiDAR (905 / 1550 nm) ~0.001 mm droplet bigger → false points, lost range
Radar 77 GHz ~4 mm droplet ~1000× smaller → almost no effect
  • Rain is different: raindrops are 1–5 mm, close to the radar wavelength, so heavy rain does attenuate and road spray adds clutter. Mine dust is mostly fine, so radar passes through it. The worst case is on the sensor: wet mud or ice on the radome.
  • Angle, worked example: 2° at 80 m ≈ 2.8 m, so two objects side by side can merge into one blob; the camera separates them easily.
  • Why stationary targets get filtered: when the truck moves, everything stationary (walls, berms, road, gantries, a boulder) appears to approach at the truck's own speed; with weak vertical resolution the radar can't tell an overhead structure from a stopped vehicle, so trackers de-prioritise stationary targets unless they were seen moving. A boulder or a broken-down dumper on a haul road is exactly that case → explicit JD "static obstacles" scenario with a detection-range criterion. Worth asking whether ARi's radar is a 4D imaging radar.

Multipath off pit walls The echo bounces wall → target → radar: a ghost target at the wrong place, or a real one at the wrong range. Tracker plausibility; test near walls specifically.
Clutter (berms, rough road, walls) Many returns; tune CFAR too low and you get false alarms, too high and you miss targets. Measure false alerts per hour and detection rate per condition; agree both in the acceptance criteria.
Blind curves Radar is line of sight: it can't see round a pit-wall bend (multipath occasionally does, unreliably). A known limit: the system must not claim coverage it doesn't have; alert timing on curves is its own scenario.
Radome blockage (mud, dust, ice) Signal attenuated; range drops or the radar goes blind. The radar's blockage detection reports degraded/blind → driver warning, degraded mode.
Misalignment (vibration shifts the bracket) Angle error: objects placed in the wrong lane or missed. Alignment monitoring (radars estimate their own misalignment from stationary targets) + a static re-alignment with a reflector after install or repair .
Interference (other trucks' radars in the band) Raised noise floor or ghost targets. Built-in interference mitigation; test with a second radar running.
Communication fault (CAN timeout, frozen counter, bad CRC) Old or corrupt objects. Receiver detects it (timeout / alive counter / CRC) → treat radar as unavailable → safe state. Same test as the ADAS-request timeout at Daimler (STAR-04).
Power dip / reset Radar reboots; seconds with no data. Detected as unavailable; warning; recovery tested.

Deeper : - Multipath: the path radar → pit wall → target → radar is longer, so the target looks farther away, or at a false position mirrored in the wall. Ground bounce makes a target seem to fade in and out with distance. Test near walls with a known target. - Clutter and the threshold (CFAR) trade-off: too low → berms and rough road trigger false alerts (drivers stop trusting it: a safety failure too); too high → small or dark targets missed. So the acceptance criteria need both numbers per condition, e.g. "person-sized target detected by X m in 99 % of runs" and "at most Y false alerts per hour". - Blind curves: the system must not claim coverage it doesn't have; this is where GNSS maps and vehicle-to-vehicle messages would help. - Radome blockage: the radar notices it's no longer getting its usual background echoes and reports blocked/degraded. Test with a mud-like layer: reported within the specified time, driver warned, safe degradation. - Communication faults: three checks in the receiving ECU: timeout (no message in time), frozen alive counter (stale data still arriving), bad CRC (corrupt data). Any one → "radar unavailable" → warning + safe state. = the ADAS-request timeout test I run on the HIL (STAR-04). - Power dip: test the recovery as well as the failure.

4a. Radar alignment with a reflector, step by step

Why: the radar's own "straight ahead" (its boresight, 0°) must match the direction the truck actually drives (its thrust line). Lateral error = distance × tan(angle): 1° is ~0.9 m at 50 m and ~1.75 m at 100 m, enough to put an obstacle in the wrong lane or outside the corridor.

Why a corner reflector: three metal plates at right angles (a trihedral) send the wave straight back to wherever it came from, over a wide range of angles. So it's a strong, point-like target of known RCS that stands out from everything else in the bay.

Static alignment (the usual workshop method): 1. Prepare the vehicle: level floor, specified tyre pressures and load, suspension settled, wheel alignment correct (the thrust line is defined by the rear axle). No metal objects in the beam path. 2. Find the vehicle's reference line: from the wheel-alignment rig, or by marking the centreline on the floor (plumb lines, lasers from the wheel hubs). 3. Place the reflector at the specified distance and height, on the centreline (or a specified offset), square to the thrust line: positioned with lasers and tape, often to millimetres. 4. Run the alignment routine with the diagnostic tool (typically a UDS RoutineControl, 0x31: see notes). The radar measures the reflector's angle, horizontally and, on some units, vertically. 5. Correct: a small error (typically within a degree or so) is stored as an electronic offset; a large one means adjusting the bracket screws and measuring again. 6. Verify and record: re-measure, clear fault codes, record the measured angle, tolerance, tool, person and date.

Vertical alignment matters too: aimed too high it looks over low obstacles; too low it sees the road as clutter. Often checked with an inclinometer on the bracket.

Dynamic alignment / online monitoring: driving past stationary objects (posts, walls), the radar knows every stationary target's Doppler should equal vehicle speed × cos(angle). If the pattern is skewed, it estimates its own misalignment, corrects small drifts and sets a fault code beyond a limit.

On a mining retrofit (Quality Lead view): vibration and knocks make re-checks a must. Put alignment in the Vehicle Inspection Checklist with a tolerance and the measured value; define the re-check triggers (after install, bracket or bumper repair, any impact, and at a maintenance interval); and prove it with an accuracy test: reflectors at known lateral offsets, compare reported positions. A retrofit may not have an OEM routine: agree the supplier's tool, fixture and tolerance up front.

6. Radar vs ultrasonic: similarities and differences

Similar: both are active sensors: they send a wave, listen for the echo and work out distance from it. Both need thresholds and filtering against noise and false echoes; both have blind zones and are sensitive to mounting and dirt; both report health (timeouts / blockage). The ideas on my chassis map one-to-one: health grading ↔ radar blockage diagnostics; the gap between sensor pings to avoid cross-talk ↔ radar interference; hysteresis and the 5-frame confirm ↔ CFAR + track confirmation; the jump filter ↔ tracker gating.

Speed Not measured (only from successive distances) Measured directly (Doppler)

Automotive camera: how it works, fog, failure modes, validation

1. How an automotive camera works

The chain: lens → image sensor → image signal processor (ISP) → video link → perception software → objects → decision.

Stage What it does What matters for validation
Lens Field of view: wide (e.g. ~120° for near-field) vs narrow (~30–50° for long range). Wider = sees more, but each object covers fewer pixels, so it's detected later.
Image sensor (CMOS) Pixels collect light for a short exposure time. Automotive sensors: typically 1–8 MP, HDR (~120 dB+, to see a dark tunnel and bright sky in one frame), LED flicker mitigation (LED lights pulse; a short exposure can catch them "off"). Rolling vs global shutter: a rolling shutter reads rows one after another, so fast motion or vibration skews the image.
ISP Turns raw sensor data into a usable image: demosaicing (colour), exposure and white-balance control, HDR merging, noise reduction, lens-distortion correction. ISP settings change what the perception sees; they're part of the configuration under test.
Video link Raw video is far too heavy for CAN, so it travels over a SerDes link (GMSL or FPD-Link), gigabits per second on one coax, to the edge computer . A "smart camera" does the perception inside and sends an object list over CAN FD or Ethernet instead. Link loss, dropped or frozen frames must be detected: frame counters and timestamps.
Perception Classical vision (colour/edge thresholds, like my HSV chassis) or, in production, neural networks (CNNs) that detect and classify objects (person, vehicle, rock), find drivable area or road edges, and estimate distance. A neural network is only as good as its training data: a model trained on city roads may miss mine objects (domain shift).
Tracking + output Confirm objects over several frames, predict motion, estimate range (mono camera: from size/position, weak; stereo: from the difference between two views, better). Distance from a single camera is an estimate, and poor far away: radar does range better .

Frame rate: typically 30 fps, i.e. a new image every ~33 ms. Latency chain: exposure → readout → ISP → link → inference (often tens of ms) → tracking (confirming over N frames adds N × 33 ms) → decision → HMI.

2. Why fog hurts cameras, and what helps

  • Physics: fog droplets (~1–20 µm) are much bigger than visible-light wavelengths (~0.5 µm), so they scatter light strongly .
  • Two effects: light from the object is scattered away (attenuation), and ambient light scattered towards the camera adds a bright veil (airlight). Both reduce contrast; colours wash out (saturation drops).
  • At night: the truck's own headlights backscatter off the fog into the camera (like high beams in fog), and oncoming headlights cause glare/blooming.
  • Dust behaves like fog; rain drops on the lens blur the image; low sun saturates it.
  • What helps: HDR and good exposure control; lens heaters and cleaning (washer, air jet); image dehazing algorithms (help a little, can't create lost information); and thermal (LWIR, 8–14 µm) cameras: longer wavelength, less scattering, and they see heat (people, engines, tyres) with no light needed. Thermal is a common choice for fog and night; worth asking whether Fog Safe Navigation uses one.

SAE J3016 levels of driving automation + the ODD

There are six levels, 0 to 5. People because Level 0 means no automation. Three questions separate them: who steers and controls speed, who watches the road, and who handles the fallback.

Level Name The car does The human does Example
0 No driving automation Warns, or steps in for a moment Drives the whole time Lane-departure warning, AEB (active safety, not Level 1)
1 Driver assistance Steering or speed Drives, watches, is the fallback ACC alone, or lane-keep alone
2 Partial automation Steering and speed Still watches and takes over instantly ACC + lane centring
3 Conditional automation Drives and watches, within limits Takes over when asked (takeover request) Mercedes Drive Pilot
4 High automation Drives, watches and handles its own fallback (a minimal risk manoeuvre), within its ODD Not needed inside the ODD Waymo robotaxis
5 Full automation Everything, everywhere a human could drive Passenger Nobody commercially

The two lines that matter: - Between Levels 2 and 3, monitoring moves to the car. - Between Levels 3 and 4, the fallback moves to the car. At Level 4 there's nobody to hand back to, so the controllers themselves must detect faults and reach a safe state. That's why validation gets so much harder at Level 4 .

The AV safety layer: doer/checker, fault detection, reaction ladder, standards

  • STAR-04: the ACC acceleration-request timeout.
  • STAR-06: CRC, counter and timeout FuSa tests.
  • STAR-14: the ESP32 chassis, where the camera only requests a stop and the controller latches it.
  • STAR-02 and STAR-11: power and grounding faults.

1. The chain

SENSORS -> high-performance computer: perception -> localisation -> planner (RL) -> SAFETY CONTROLLER -> drive-by-wire ECUs -> actuators
           (GPU, Linux, many processes over IPC) envelope check, (CAN / CAN FD) steering, brake,
                                                                         owns the safe stop throttle / e-drive
           <-------- feedback: wheel speeds, steering angle, brake pressure, IMU -------- + a watchdog in both directions

Why the safety layer is separate (the "doer / checker" pattern): 1. An RL policy can't be fully specified or verified, but a rule can. "Never more than X°/s of steering at Y km/h", or "planner silent for 100 ms → stop", can be written down and tested. 2. The big computer is the part most likely to fail (Linux, GPUs, heat), so the part that can stop the vehicle can't live on it. 3. The planner proposes; the safety layer decides.

The safety layer also needs its own sensing (wheel speeds, IMU) and its own power, or one fault takes out both. Mobileye's RSS is one published example of a checker.

2. Detecting faults, link by link (sized to the FTTI)

Link Fault Detected by
Sensor → computer Dead or unplugged Timeout: no new frames
Wrong but alive (glare, dirt) Cross-checks between sensors, plausibility ranges, frozen values (SOTIF territory)
Inside the computer A process crashes or freezes; stale data; a cycle overruns Heartbeats and a supervisor; timestamps with a maximum age; execution-time monitoring
Computer → safety controller Commands stop, or arrive corrupted, repeated, out of order or unsafe A timeout; E2E CRC + counter + data ID ; an envelope check; a window or question-and-answer watchdog
Safety controller → ECUs Bus failure Bus-off, error counters, a timeout in each ECU
ECU → actuator The actuator doesn't follow Commanded vs measured (steering angle, deceleration), with the gap held for N ms
Power A dip or brownout Undervoltage monitoring; a separate supply for the safety controller

The FTTI decides the timeouts. The FTTI (ISO 26262) runs from a fault to possible harm, and detection plus reaction must fit inside it. Example: at 30 km/h a vehicle covers about 8 m/s. If a bad steering command would leave the lane in 300 ms, detecting and reacting must take less than 300 ms. Tuning a timeout is the debounce trade-off: a short one is fast but trips falsely; a long one is robust but slow.

Watchdogs: - A simple watchdog catches a total hang, but not a computer stuck in a loop that keeps kicking it. - A window watchdog (the kick must come neither too early nor too late) or a question-and-answer watchdog (the computer must answer a challenge) also catches a computer that's alive but running wrong. That's the usual choice for safety controllers.
